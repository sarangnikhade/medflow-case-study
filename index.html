06
Research Methods

Qualitative vs. quantitative

Method A

Qualitative

What it answers

“Why do people skip medications?”

Data type

Stories, observations, emotions — the texture of lived experience

Sample size

Small — 5 to 10 participants is enough for depth

Methods
User Interviews Contextual Inquiry Diary Studies
Limitation

Not statistically representative — can’t generalise to millions

Method B

Quantitative

What it answers

“How many people skip medications?”

Data type

Numbers, frequencies, patterns — measurable and comparable

Sample size

Large — hundreds or thousands for statistical significance

Methods
Surveys Analytics A/B Testing
Limitation

Tells you what happened — not why. Can’t explain motivations.

The golden rule: always qualitative first

In discovery, run interviews before surveys. Without understanding the why, your survey answer options will embed your assumptions — and you’ll get confirmation of whatever you already believed.

07
Usability Testing

Moderated, unmoderated, and think-aloud

Moderated

In-person or video call

A researcher is present and can ask follow-up questions in real time. Best for complex tasks, early prototypes, or sensitive topics where you need to understand why someone behaves a certain way.

Use when

Tasks are complex · emotional/sensitive topic · you need “why?” probing · early prototype stage

Unmoderated

Remote · self-guided

Participants complete tasks alone, with their screen and voice recorded. Faster and cheaper to run at scale. Good when the prototype is stable enough that users won’t get irretrievably stuck.

Use when

Prototype is stable · tasks are clear and finite · need fast results · large sample

Think-Aloud Protocol

Ask users to narrate their thinking while they work

As a facilitator your only rule: stay silent. Don’t offer hints, don’t finish their sentences, don’t nod encouragingly when they go wrong. The moment you help, you’ve invalidated the test.

Golden rule
Don’t give hints or UI direction
Don’t react to their struggles
Ask “what are you thinking?” not “do you see this button?”
Test Tasks — MedFlow Prototype
T1 Medication setup

“You’ve just been prescribed a Thyroid medication. Add it to your routine.”

Why this works

Scenario-based, no UI hints. Tests onboarding flow and schedule setup without leading the user.

T2 Refill flow

“You’re almost out of your medication. Reorder it.”

Why this works

Targets a critical drop-off point — running out. No feature names, just a real-world situation.

08
Survey Design

5 ways surveys go wrong

Double-barreled

Failure 1

Asks two questions in one. The respondent can only give one answer, making it impossible to know which question they’re answering.

Bad example

“Is the app easy to use and does it remind you on time?”

Leading question

Failure 2

Nudges respondents toward a specific answer. Injects your hypothesis into the question itself and produces biased data.

Bad example

“How much did the reminders help you remember?”

Loaded assumption

Failure 3

Embeds an untested assumption inside the question. Forces respondents to accept something as true before they can answer.

Bad example

“When you forget your medication, what usually causes it?”

Survey before interviews

Failure 4

Running a survey in discovery — before you know what to measure — means your answer options reflect your assumptions, not users’ reality.

The problem

You’re measuring the wrong things at the wrong time

Wrong sample

Failure 5

Sending your survey to the wrong people. If you’re designing for patients with chronic conditions and you survey healthy 20-year-olds, your data is useless — regardless of sample size.

The rule

1,000 wrong people < 10 right people

09
Contextual Inquiry

What people say vs. what they actually do

Interview (self-report)

What they SAY

People describe their behaviour from memory — filtered, edited, often inaccurate. They tell you the ideal version of themselves, not the reality.

“I always take my morning pill with breakfast.”

Reality: grabbed it 20 minutes later on the way out the door

Contextual Inquiry

What they DO

You observe users in their natural environment, at the actual moment the behaviour happens. No memory bias, no edited version — just reality.

Observer sees the pillbox on top of the coffee maker — not at the breakfast table.

Insight: cues already exist — design needs to anchor on them

Why habitual behaviours are worst for self-report

Habitual and unconscious behaviours — taking a pill, locking the door, checking a phone — happen below the level of conscious awareness. People can’t accurately report what they don’t notice. This is exactly why contextual inquiry matters for a medication app: the most important moments (skipping a dose, forgetting a refill) are the ones users are least able to describe in an interview.

Jobs-to-be-Done

What users are really trying to accomplish

Format
When [situation] I want to [motivation — no solution] So I can [desired outcome]
01 Lunch with client
When

I go out for a client lunch meeting,

I want to

track whether I’ve taken my dose,

So I can

focus on my client without medication anxiety in the back of my mind.

02 Evening at home
When

I finish dinner and sit down to watch TV,

I want to

know I haven’t missed any critical night-time medications,

So I can

relax and enjoy my evening without confusion or doubt.

03 Family dinner out
When

I go out for dinner with my family,

I want to

keep track of medications that need to be taken before or after eating,

So I can

enjoy dinner with my family without health anxiety running in the background.

04 Work travel
When

I travel out of the city for work,

I want to

make sure I don’t miss a refill before I leave,

So I can

focus on my work while travelling without any worry about running out.

The “I want to” line must never contain a product feature or solution — only the human motivation. The moment you write “I want a notification,” you’ve jumped from need to solution and lost the insight.
